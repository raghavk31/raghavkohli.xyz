## 00 the commitment

The Climate Resilient City Action Plan committed Ahmedabad to raising its green cover from
6.8 square metres per person to the WHO's nine. The Urban Green Policy 2030 is how. Its
objectives are plain: restore nature within urban spaces, connect the green areas, bring nature
into parks, gardens and private plots, and get the community to take part. It is written
against the frameworks the corporation has signed up to: SDGs 11, 13, 14, 15 and 17, the
Kunming–Montreal biodiversity targets, the national ones.

What sets it apart is the first challenge it names, inequitable distribution. The centre has
the big green projects; the periphery does not.

## 01 baseline

The baseline chapter is where the design earned its keep. Tree cover is about 60 square
kilometres, 12.5 per cent of the city, and it moved between 2012 and 2024: up in Bhaipura
Hatkeshwar, Indrapuri and Kubernagar, down in Vatva, Lambha and Amraiwadi. Land surface
temperature drawn against tree cover shows the hottest ground is the barest. Park access at 400,
800 and 3,000 metres reaches 83.5 per cent of the city and misses Gota, Nikol, Vatva, Narol,
Lambha and Sarkhej.

The book draws all of this ward by ward, because that is the scale the policy's committees will
work at. A city-wide percentage hides a ward; the ward map does not.

## 02 three greens

The policy sorts the city's green into three kinds and gives each an owner: avenues, medians and
junctions, under AMC, AUDA, the highways and public works; public greens (parks, gardens,
forests, the riverfront) under the corporation; private greens, from institutional campuses to
balconies, under whoever holds the plot. For the street, the rules are drawn to scale: the size of
tree to the width of the verge, the section from building to carriageway, the criteria for
choosing a native species over an ornamental one.

## 03 catalogue

Then the catalogue: urban forests and Miyawaki plantations, riverfront and open-space planting,
biodiversity parks, the open spaces inside residential complexes, parking lots, rooftops,
institutional grounds, vertical gardens, kitchen gardens. Each gets its guidelines and its
illustration on the same spread, so that the rule and the picture of the rule are never on
different pages.

## 04 care

A heritage tree is a tree with a story: age, size, form, a rare species, a festival held under
it. The policy sets out how one is recognised and protected. The maintenance chapter is
practical: how to prune, where to apply for permission, how to mulch, how to manage pests with
neem and lime rather than chemicals, and how to keep a tree health card (identification,
inspection, growth, soil, records) for every tree the corporation plants.

## 05 people and money

The existing garden ward committees become Ward-level Urban Green Committees, with residents,
experts, youth and women on them, planning their ward's greening and keeping the health cards.
An Urban Green Fund takes public and private money and every penalty. Felling needs permission,
is never permitted for red-listed or reserved species or for trees under seven years old, and
costs a fee per tree that goes to the fund. Green waste gets a helpline, composting centres and a
monthly training target. The chapter closes with what Mumbai, Salem, Bengaluru and Bhubaneswar
already do.

## 06 the greens

Between the chapters, the city's own gardens from above: the riverfront terraces, the oxygen
parks, the domed garden, the planted junctions. They are there to remind the reader that the
policy is not starting from nothing: 275 urban forests and oxygen parks in five years, three
million trees planted in 2024, and a survival rate of sixty per cent that the policy exists to
raise.

## 07 annexures

The annexures are the working end: a native species list with vernacular, scientific and IUCN
status, so a committee plants what belongs; and the application form itself, designed rather
than photocopied, because the form is the policy's most-used page.
