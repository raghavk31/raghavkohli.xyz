## 00 the city

Mumbai was seven islands, and it became one city by filling the sea between them: hills
quarried and levelled at a rate that alarmed even the engineers doing it. The fishing villages
were there before the filling started and are still there after it — pinned to the 1930 map,
still on the 2022 one. The timeline the thesis opens with is the story of how the land the
Kolis worked, the intertidal zone between water and ground, was recategorised as wasteland
so that it could be reclaimed.

## 01 the kolis

The Kolis are the artisanal fishing community and the first inhabitants of the city — five
hundred years on this shore. A koliwada is the dense gaothan of houses and the commons around
it: the drying grounds, the creek, the mangroves, the intertidal flats where creek fishing with
small nets kept the families who could not afford boats. It is a **highly productive landscape**
that the city's maps read as empty, and the interface between it and the towers around it is
Mumbai's characteristic adjacency.

## 02 exclusion

The means of exclusion are ordinary documents. The Coastal Road reclaims 10.5 kilometres of the
western shore through the very waters the villages fish; the coastal zone regulations were
written for the shore as a hazard, not a workplace; the development plan reads the koliwadas as
slum. The thesis's first job was to lay these out plainly — the DP, the CRZ, the ICZM, the
project reports — and to draw what they do to a village's access to the sea.

## 03 trajectory

If the usual continues, it ends underwater. The projection to 2050 puts a metre or more of sea
on the reclaimed ground, and the people it displaces first are the ones on the shore. The
collage is blunt because the numbers are: land lost and people affected at one, two, three
metres, against a city still filling the sea.

## 04 worli

Worli Koliwada is eight hundred years old and sits on one of the seven original islands, the
Arabian Sea on one side and Mahim Bay on the other, with the Bandra–Worli sea link landing at
its northern tip. It was chosen because it is at the fringe of extreme development pressure and
well on its way to succumbing to it — and because, read closely, it is **an open repository of
exchange**: of fish, of nets, of festivals, of knowledge.

## 05 commons

The method was to identify the local capacities, resources and opportunities of a place before
proposing anything for it. Every common space in the village was drawn to scale, from a
ten-square-metre shrine court to the thirty-thousand-square-metre tip, and four of them were
chosen to study in the round: the Worli tip, the east jetty, the maidan, and the linkage where
the village becomes the city.

## 06 01 worli tip

The tip is the village's working floor. From the jetty at the northern point the boats leave at
four in the morning; through the day the open ground is fish sorting, cleaning, drying and
selling to the fisher women who carry it to the market and the city; and on Narali Poornima,
Holi and Ganesh Chaturthi it is the stage. Moving south the levels change and play takes over.
The temple, the church, the fort, the resource repositories, the boats, the mangroves — the
cartography pins each to where it was photographed, because **the drawing's argument is that all
of this happens without anyone having designed it.**

## 07 02 east jetty

On the bay side, a new jetty and its temple, then community bins and toilets and a playground,
then a run of dilapidated piers. The whole edge is where the village's waste enters the sea —
and where the village stacks that waste in compact bags as a barrier against the high tide.
Ragpickers work the edge; children play at the water; bamboo structures come and go. Waste,
water, play, piers and boats are the elements the thesis takes from here.

## 08 03 maidan

Inside the village, a chain of open spaces of every scale. The maidan itself, overlooking the
coast, where play is the medium through which different people negotiate the same ground. The
mitra mandals — the community groups, each with a semi-open room in its own sub-community. The
temple courtyards. The small education and sport centres. The thesis reads these as **knowledge
sharing artefacts**: the spatial types through which the village teaches itself.

## 09 04 linkage

Where the village street meets the secondary street and the city, the livelihoods change: car
mechanics, silk work, carpenters, an informal market edge. The canal that was once the way to
Worli island is an open drain. Toward the city are the high-rises where, as fishing declines,
the village works as security guards and rickshaw drivers. This seam is where the thesis wants
to increase the flux the other way — the village's capacities flowing into the city, not only
its labour.

## 10 what if

The proposals are offered as initiations, not answers, at two scales. At the neighbourhood
scale, a strategic toolkit drawn from what the village already has — boats, nets, mangroves,
bamboo, waste, water, food, fish, the levels of the shore — set against the four responses to
sea-level rise: accommodate, advance, retreat, protect. At the city scale, the urban villages and
informal settlements as **eco-punctures**: the places where Mumbai's ecological and economic
resilience could be grown from the ground, linked into a knowledge landscape rather than cleared
for one. The contribution is a change of direction — the city as a ground of open opportunities,
and its most vulnerable people as the creative drivers of collective change.
