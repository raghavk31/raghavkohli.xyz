## 00 the city

A climate action plan is only as good as the picture of the city it starts from. This one
starts from a single municipality in a single year — Ahmedabad, 2021–22 — and refuses to
generalise beyond it. Population, wards, the development plan already in force, the
utilities that keep the city running: the plan is written for the people who run those
things, in the units they use.

The book is one of three in the CapaCities series, with Surat and Vadodara — the same
method, applied here to a city of seven million.

## 01 the atlas

Before the plan proposes anything, it draws. Twenty-seven maps at ward level, in three
passes. First the **infrastructure** as it stands: water, wastewater, stormwater, waste,
transport, parks, fire cover, air. Then the **hazards**: the heat map for May, the flood
zone, the pollution hotspots — and the slums and vendors sitting inside each. Then the
cross: hazard laid over infrastructure, ward by ward, to find where a heatwave meets a
water shortage, where a flood meets a broken road, where an emergency service is itself
inside the flood zone.

The atlas is the analytical core of the plan. Everything that follows — the targets, the
actions, the costs — points back to a ward on one of these maps.

## 02 the inventory

The maps say where; the inventory says how much. Every activity number has a named
source — the electricity utilities, the oil and gas companies, the corporation's own
fleet and plants, the railway, the airport — and the sum is a number the city can be
held to: **15.09 million tonnes of CO₂e in a year**, 2.1 tonnes per person, nearly half
of it from manufacturing and construction, a quarter from homes.

Five years of the same accounting, 2017–18 to 2021–22, give the trend: 13.4% up, 2.7% a
year, with the pandemic dip in between. That trend is the business-as-usual line the
rest of the plan argues against.

## 03 the pathway

Three lines to 2070. Business as usual, rising past a hundred million tonnes. The
**progressive action scenario** — every scheme and target already on the books at the
state and the centre, pushed as far as they go — which cuts 76% from BAU and still leaves
the city emitting more in 2070 than it does today. And the **net-zero pathway**, which
extends each of those actions until the remaining line meets the target: 55.7% below BAU
in 2030, 79.2% in 2050, 91% in 2070.

The chart carries the argument. Each coloured band is one action's contribution; the
width of the band is its ambition. Buildings — efficient by 2070, green by 2050, running
on renewables — do most of the work.

## 04 strategies

Eight sectors, each with one goal, a handful of strategies, and targets by decade. The
goals are plain and measurable: a heat-resilient, sustainable-energy city; 80% of trips
on foot, cycle or public transport; 100% wastewater treated and reused; a zero-waste city;
the National Clean Air Programme met. My part of the book begins here — the strategies
were drawn before they were tabulated, so that a department head could see the piece of
city each one changes.

## 05 actions

Forty-five action sheets, one format throughout. An isometric of the intervention on the
left; on the right its target, the entity that implements it, the financing mode, the
indicative cost and timeline, and a resilience score. The sheets are the part of the
plan that gets picked up: a sheet is what a department takes to a budget meeting.
