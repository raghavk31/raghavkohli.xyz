The studio brief opened with a number: rural migration and urban growth will demand 3,000 new
cities in the next twenty to thirty years. The question was whether a neighbourhood can take
high density without giving up spatial quality, climate, access to open space and a mix of uses,
and whether it can do that while keeping every basic amenity in walking distance.

The two answers already in use both fail. Unplanned growth spills past the city boundary and
becomes sprawl. Planned densification stacks floors on the same footprint and calls it a
solution. The studio's bet was that density is an opportunity if it is designed as a set of
relationships, between built and open, people and space, rather than as a fixed drawing.

## 00 the problem

That is a lot to hold in one plan, so the studio did not draw one. It wrote simple rules for how
things should relate, coded them into a computational model, and built the complexity up a rule
at a time. Evolutionary algorithms then generated the permutations those rules allow and searched
them for the fittest.

## 01 site

The site is in the central north of Ahmedabad, on the Sabarmati: 1.45 square kilometres tied
into the larger city by bus and metro stops and by primary and secondary junctions. Inside it,
several networks overlap: transport, interconnectivity, the street network, and an identity
network of places that matter.

Those places are what the site is. A green patch, Kalam Kush, Gandhi Ashram, a sewage treatment
plant, the river and its riverfront, and a swale. The section explains the swale: the ground falls
from upland to shoreline, and before it was developed the site worked as a retention basin.

## 02 the workshop

The method came first, on a test block, in an evolutionary computation workshop. The plot was
constant, 200 by 120 metres. The residential block offset, the division into units, the
staggering between them, the depth of the shops, the number of floors, the scaling of the
terraces: each was given a range rather than a value. Those ranges are the genepool.

Against them sit the fitness criteria: maximise open space on ground and rooftops, maximise shade
in the plaza, maximise sunlight in the circulation corridors, maximise built volume. The solver
breeds populations of blocks, scores each one, and keeps the Pareto front, the individuals no other
block beats on every objective. The radars show the trade-off directly: a block that wins on
density loses on open space. There is no single best answer, only a set of compromises, and the
designer chooses among them with the reasons visible.

Written this way, design intent can be argued with. You can disagree with a rule precisely, in a
way you cannot disagree with a rendering.

## 03 how much to build

The arithmetic came before any form. In 2025 the site holds 65,873 people, needs 16,46,825 square
metres of built space, and fits in four floors. By 2035 it is 92,921 people and seven floors. In
2050 it is 1,55,676 people, 38,91,900 square metres built and 14,01,084 of open space, which is more
open space than there is site. Put all of it on the ground and the buildings reach 151 floors.

Move 5,64,795 square metres of that open space onto rooftops, and they come down to twelve. That
one comparison set the ambition for the rest of the project: a liveable neighbourhood that stays
liveable as it densifies, with open space on more levels than the ground.

## 04 what to keep

Before any rule was written, the site was read for what it should not lose. The treatment plant,
Gandhi Ashram, the bio-swale and Kalam Kush, 63,350 square metres together, are retained; 1.38 of
the 1.45 square kilometres is left to develop.

The retained places then become low-density attractors, and the transit stops, junctions and the
TOD edge become high-density ones. Density is not zoned. It falls out of distance: closest to
transit, the most people; closest to what must be kept, the fewest.

## 05 three horizons

So the density distribution is not one drawing but three. The same attractors run at 2025, 2035
and 2050, the high-density radii widening from 150 to 250 metres around the stops while the
low-density radii around the treatment plant and Kalam Kush tighten from 100 to 50. The
high-density share of the built area moves from a quarter to 43 per cent. The density map is a function of time
rather than a fixed zoning.

## 06 desire lines

The street network was not drawn either. Each kind of destination was taken on its own: street
nodes, homes, shops, social amenities, green spaces, transit stops. Its nodes were joined by
direct lines across the site, and the lines were bundled where they ran together.

Six sets of bundled lines, laid over each other, show where movement across the site wants to go.
The primary network was drawn from that overlap.

## 07 the network

The foreground network connects the north–south and east–west points of the site and takes in
Gandhi Ashram, the treatment plant and Kalam Kush. A space syntax betweenness analysis of the
result shows more choice of movement, less traffic load near Gandhi Ashram, and direct
connectivity across the site.

Behind it sits a background network of secondary and tertiary streets, a grid woven at 130 metres.
Each strand carries a different mobility type, so the grid can support all of them equally
without any one degrading the rest.

## 08 land use

Land use follows the same attractors as density. Residential, at 85 per cent, is dense toward the
TOD edge, the stops and the major nodes, and thin along the treatment plant, Kalam Kush and
Gandhi Ashram. Commercial, at 25 per cent, is high at the stops, the major nodes and the primary
junction, medium along the primary network, and low up to 100 metres from a primary node.

Open and green space is 35 per cent. The green patch along the swale is kept, and the rest is
spread evenly across the site.

## 09 the precedent

Ciudad Bachué in Bogotá was the precedent, a planned settlement whose houses were designed to be
extended. Residents extended them further. Horizontally, onto community streets and adjacent open
space, into the internal backyard, and by pushing each upper floor 30 to 60 centimetres past the
wall below. Vertically, by two storeys on average. Some house types grew to 280 per cent of their
first size.

The lesson for the typologies was that incrementality is not a failure of the plan. It is a use
of the building the plan should make room for, so it became a fitness criterion of its own.

## 10 typologies

The building types come from the same method. Four primitives, single courtyard, L, U and tower,
are given residential and commercial body plans. For residential type 1 the genes are a grid of
3 to 7.5 metres, a depth of 12 to 18, height from 0 to 24, voids of 36 to 180 cubic metres, and
staggering at a factor of 1 to 1.5. The fitness criteria are shade in the courtyard, built volume,
sunlight on the rooftops, height difference, and open space on the ground and rooftops.

Eight typologies come out of one rule set, from a coarse perimeter block to towers on a mat, each
with the grid, plates and cells that generate it drawn beside it. What looks like eight designs is
one intention, resolved eight ways.

## 11 the search

For type 1 the procedure is written out as pseudo-code (a 60-metre block, an offset for the court,
a division into cells, plates stacked at three metres, cells culled at eight points to make voids,
non-uniform scaling, extrusion) and run for four generations. Every individual is scored on eight
criteria and drawn beside its radar.

The inferences are the kind a masterplan cannot give. Blocks with larger unit cells produce larger
open spaces and more access points, are more porous, and suit commerce and mixed use. Blocks with
smaller cells produce private, close-knit voids the size of a community gathering, with access
under the units, and suit housing. One individual, G 4.03, sits at the bottom of every criterion at
once, which is its own kind of information.

## 12 the street

What the rules produce, at eye level, is a brick neighbourhood where the voids culled from the mass
are the shaded streets and courts, and the staggered plates are the terraces that carry the open
space the ground could not. It is the earliest project on this site where the thing designed is a
system rather than a place.
